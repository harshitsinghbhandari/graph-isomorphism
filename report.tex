\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report documents a graph isomorphism (GI) pipeline that relaxes the
discrete permutation search to a quadratic program over the Birkhoff polytope,
adds a degree-profile cost to the adjacency commutator objective, rounds the
relaxed matrix with a Goemans--Williamson-style hyperplane method, and accepts
an isomorphism only when the rounded permutation passes the exact integer check
$A\hat{P} = \hat{P}B$.  The central result is intentionally narrow: the
relaxation gives an interpretable soft correspondence matrix, the degree-profile
term improves separation over an adjacency-only objective, and the current
bottleneck is clearly located in rounding quality and solver scaling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Given two simple undirected graphs $G_A$ and $G_B$ with $n$ vertices, let
$A, B \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ be their adjacency matrices in deterministic
(sorted) node order.  The graphs are isomorphic if there exists a permutation
matrix $P \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ap_pb}
  AP = PB.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $P^\top AP = B$.  The direct search space contains $n!$
permutations.  Our approach replaces that discrete search with a continuous
relaxation, then tries to recover a discrete certificate.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{graph_perm_example.png}
\caption{A graph isomorphism represented as a permutation matrix.  The graphs
look different because the node labels have been permuted, but the permutation
matrix $P$ certifies the identity $AP = PB$.}
\label{fig:graph_perm_example}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{pipeline.png}
\caption{End-to-end pipeline: convert graphs to adjacency matrices, solve the
relaxed QP over the Birkhoff polytope, round the relaxed solution, and accept
only after the exact certificate check $A\hat{P} = \hat{P}B$.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\section{Baseline Algorithms}\label{sec:baselines}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

As preliminary work before the optimization solver, classical polynomial-time
algorithms were implemented and benchmarked.

\paragraph{AHU Tree Isomorphism.}
Find tree centroids via iterative leaf removal, root at centroids, then label
bottom-up: $\operatorname{label}(v) =
\operatorname{hash}(\operatorname{sorted}\{\operatorname{label}(c) : c \in
\text{children}(v)\})$.  A \emph{shared} mapping across both trees ensures
labels match iff subtrees are identical.  Complexity: $O(n)$.  Benchmarked on
random trees up to $n = 10{,}000$.

\paragraph{Weinberg's Planar Canonical Code.}
Compute a planar embedding (rotation system), then for each starting edge
perform a DFS following the rotation, recording a code sequence.  The
lexicographically smallest code is the canonical form.  Two planar graphs are
isomorphic iff their canonical codes match.  Complexity: $O(n^2)$.  Benchmarked
across 6 graph families (paths, cycles, ladders, grids, wheels, random planar)
up to $n = 200$.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The GI problem occupies a rare complexity niche: it is not known to be in P nor
proven NP-complete.  Babai (2016) showed it is solvable in quasi-polynomial
time $2^{O((\log n)^c)}$.  For restricted graph classes, polynomial-time
algorithms exist:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Graph Class} & \textbf{Algorithm} & \textbf{Complexity} & \textbf{Ref} \\
\midrule
Trees          & AHU            & $O(n \log n)$ & Aho et al., 1974 \\
Planar Graphs  & Weinberg       & $O(n^2)$      & Weinberg, 1966 \\
Bounded-Degree & Luks           & $O(n^{O(d)})$ & Luks, 1982 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Polynomial-time GI algorithms for special graph classes.}
\end{table}

We implemented AHU and Weinberg as baselines.  The primary theoretical
foundation for the optimization approach is Klus \& Ge\ss{} (2025), who
formulate GI as a QP over the Birkhoff polytope and introduce the Isomorphism
Similarity Index.  They define \textbf{friendly graphs} - those with all
distinct eigenvalues and eigenvectors having nonzero inner product with the
all-ones vector - for which the relaxation has at most one solution.

\section{Relaxation}\label{sec:relaxation}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is \emph{doubly stochastic} if
\begin{equation}
  X_{ij} \geq 0, \qquad
  \sum_j X_{ij} = 1 \;\;\forall\, i, \qquad
  \sum_i X_{ij} = 1 \;\;\forall\, j.
\end{equation}

The set of all doubly stochastic matrices is the Birkhoff polytope
$\mathcal{B}_n$.  Making $\mathcal{B}_n$ the natural continuous
relaxation of the discrete permutation set.

In the solver, $X$ is interpreted as a \emph{soft node-correspondence matrix}:
$X_{ij}$ large means vertex $i$ in $G_A$ is being softly matched to vertex $j$
in $G_B$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{birkhoff_relaxation.png}
\caption{The Birkhoff polytope relaxes permutation matrices to doubly
stochastic matrices.  A permutation matrix is a vertex; the relaxed solution
$X^*$ can lie in the interior and contain fractional assignment weights.}
\label{fig:birkhoff_relaxation}
\end{figure}

\section{Implemented Objective}\label{sec:objective}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The solver optimizes exactly two terms:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:full_obj}
  \min_{X \in \mathcal{B}_n} \;
  w_C \langle C, X \rangle + w_Q \|AX - XB\|_F^2
\end{equation}
with default weights $w_C = 1.0$, $w_Q = 1.0$.


\subsection{Adjacency Commutator}

The term
\begin{equation}
  Q(X) = \|AX - XB\|_F^2
\end{equation}
is the continuous version of the exact condition $AP = PB$.  If $X = P$ is a
correct isomorphism, then $Q(P) = 0$.  This is the cleanest baseline objective
because it is directly tied to the definition of graph isomorphism.

\subsection{Degree-Profile Cost}

The solver builds a node-pair cost matrix from degree information.  Let
\begin{equation}
  d_i^A = \sum_k A_{ik}, \qquad d_j^B = \sum_k B_{jk}
\end{equation}
be vertex degrees, and let
\begin{equation}
  s_i^A = \sum_k A_{ik}\, d_k^A, \qquad
  s_j^B = \sum_k B_{jk}\, d_k^B
\end{equation}
be one-hop neighbour-degree summaries.  The raw cost is
\begin{equation}
  C^{\text{raw}}_{ij} = \alpha\,|d_i^A - d_j^B| + \beta\,|s_i^A - s_j^B|,
  \qquad \alpha = 1.0,\;\; \beta = 0.35.
\end{equation}

The code normalizes this cost matrix by its maximum absolute value when that
maximum is nonzero.  This matters because $\langle C, X \rangle$ and
$\|AX - XB\|_F^2$ scale differently with $n$.  Without normalization, one
term can dominate for reasons unrelated to isomorphism quality.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{objective_heatmaps.png}
\caption{Objective diagnostics from a saved solver run: the normalized
degree-profile cost matrix $C$, the relaxed correspondence $X^*$, the rounded

as a black box.}
\label{fig:objective_heatmaps}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Convexity}

The current objective is a convex quadratic program.  The adjacency term is
the squared Frobenius norm of a linear map in $X$ - hence convex.  The
degree-profile term is linear.  The feasible region $\mathcal{B}_n$ is convex.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Why Not Use Only $\|AX - XB\|_F^2$?}\label{sec:why_degree}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The adjacency-only objective is mathematically clean, but practically weak.
In relaxed space, many fractional assignments can produce small adjacency
residuals without committing to a sharp vertex correspondence.

The degree-profile term supplies low-cost local information.  It discourages
matching vertices whose degree and neighbour-degree summaries are incompatible.
It does not solve graph isomorphism by itself, but it improves the shape of the
optimization landscape.

The question for this work is therefore:

\begin{center}
\emph{Does adding $\langle C, X \rangle$ improve separation between isomorphic
and non-isomorphic cases without increasing runtime materially?}
\end{center}

Current experiments support this direction.  For small reruns, isomorphic pairs
still have objective values close to zero, while verified non-isomorphic random
pairs receive much larger objective values when the degree-profile cost is
enabled.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{index_ablation.png}
\caption{Same generated graph pairs.  With only
$\|AX-XB\|_F^2$, random pairs can still receive high index values, so the
relaxed score is weakly separated.  Adding the degree-profile term keeps
isomorphic cases near $I=1$ while pushing random-pair scores downward.}
\label{fig:index_ablation}
\end{figure}

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Isomorphism Index}\label{sec:index}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

After optimization, the solver reports the objective value $Z^*$ and a bounded
similarity score
\begin{equation}
  I = \exp(-\lambda Z^*), \qquad \lambda = 0.1 \text{ (default)}.
\end{equation}

This index is useful for plots and experiment summaries.  It is \textbf{not} a
proof.  The correct interpretation is:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $I \approx 1$: the relaxed objective found a very low-cost soft
        correspondence.
  \item $I \ll 1$: the relaxed objective found substantial mismatch.
  \item Neither statement is an exact isomorphism decision.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rounding}\label{sec:rounding}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The relaxed solution $X^*$ is generally fractional.  To obtain a candidate
isomorphism, the solver calls \texttt{hyperplane\_rounding.py}.

\subsection{Hyperplane Rounding Path}

The current rounding path is:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Compute an SVD embedding of rows and columns of $X^*$.
  \item Sample random hyperplanes in the embedding space.
  \item Convert projections into sign signatures.
  \item Build a Hamming-distance cost matrix between row and column signatures.
  \item Solve a linear assignment problem to obtain a permutation candidate.
  \item Repeat for multiple trials and keep the permutation with the smallest
        integer residual.
\end{enumerate}

Formally: the SVD $X^* = U\Sigma V^T$ yields row embeddings
$U_e = U_{[:,:r]}\sqrt{\Sigma_{[:r]}}$ and column embeddings
$V_e = V_{[:,:r]}\sqrt{\Sigma_{[:r]}}$ such that $X^*_{ij} \approx
\langle u_i, v_j \rangle$.  For each trial, a Gaussian matrix
$G \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ defines random hyperplanes.  The sign
signatures are $S_A = \operatorname{sign}(U_e G)$ and $S_B =
\operatorname{sign}(V_e G)$.  The Hamming-distance cost matrix
$\operatorname{cost}_{ij} = (k - S_A[i,:] \cdot S_B[j,:]) / 2$ feeds the
Hungarian algorithm.

The implementation runs 200 trials by default with $k = \lceil 4\log_2(n+2)
\rceil$ hyperplanes per trial.  It stops early if any trial gives zero
residual---an exact isomorphism witness.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{rounding_diagram.png}
\caption{Hyperplane rounding converts the SVD embeddings of rows and columns
of $X^*$ into sign signatures.  The row and column signatures define a
Hamming-distance assignment problem, whose solution is a candidate permutation.}
\label{fig:rounding_diagram}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Relation to Max-Cut}

The inspiration comes from Goemans--Williamson (1995) rounding for Max-Cut: a
continuous geometric solution is converted to a discrete object by random
hyperplanes.  Here the role is different.  We are not cutting one vertex set
into two sides.  We are using random hyperplane \emph{signatures} to convert
the two SVD embeddings from $X^*$ into a discrete matching problem.

\subsection{Hungarian Baseline}

Hungarian rounding remains an important internal baseline.  Given $X^*$, it
directly finds a high-weight permutation under the assignment cost $1 - X^*$.

In current small tests, hyperplane rounding has not yet shown a clear
certificate advantage over the Hungarian baseline.  That is not a failure of
the method; it is a useful experimental result -the value of hyperplane
rounding may become apparent on harder graph families where $X^*$ is more
diffuse.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Exact Verification}\label{sec:verification}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The final decision is made only after rounding.  Given a candidate permutation
$\hat{P}$, the code checks
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cert}
  A\hat{P} = \hat{P}B
\end{equation}
using integer-valued matrices.

If the equality holds, $\hat{P}$ is a \emph{certificate} of isomorphism.  If it
does not hold, the result is ``not certified.''  This is the key distinction:

\begin{center}
\fbox{\textbf{Failed certificate $\neq$ proof of non-isomorphism.}}
\end{center}

\noindent The current method can prove isomorphism when rounding succeeds.  It
does \emph{not} prove non-isomorphism when rounding fails.

This is currently the main engineering bottleneck.

\section{Failure Modes}\label{sec:failures}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Diffuse Relaxed Solutions}

Graphs with symmetry or weak objective identifiability can produce diffuse
solutions.  For example, a cycle graph can allow the uniform matrix
\begin{equation}
  X_{ij} = \frac{1}{n}
\end{equation}
to satisfy the relaxed objective extremely well.  That matrix contains no sharp
permutation information.  The deeper issue is not only symmetry: the objective
may have multiple equivalent or near-equivalent minimisers.  When the degree
cost is weak and the adjacency term has a wide valley, rounding can fail even
if an exact permutation exists.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{diffuse_vs_sharp.png}
\caption{Rounding difficulty depends on how much permutation information is
present in $X^*$.  A flat interior point contains almost no assignment signal,
an ambiguous relaxed solution contains partial signal, and a sharp permutation
matrix directly identifies a candidate isomorphism.}
\label{fig:diffuse_vs_sharp}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Weak Cost Matrix}

Degree and neighbour-degree summaries are cheap but incomplete.  Regular
graphs, strongly regular graphs, and many structured families can share the
same low-order profiles.  On those cases, $C$ may add little or no
information.  A natural next step is to add stronger node features such as
Weisfeiler--Leman colour refinement features.

\subsection{Term Scaling}

The linear degree term and quadratic adjacency term do not scale the same way.
The code now normalises $C$, but a full normalisation strategy should also
consider the typical magnitude of $\|AX - XB\|_F^2$ as $n$ changes.

\subsection{Rounding Is Heuristic}

Hyperplane rounding can miss a valid isomorphism even when one exists.  The
result is then inconclusive, not a proof of non-isomorphism.

\subsection{Random-Pair Labels}

Random independently sampled graph pairs are almost surely non-isomorphic for
moderate $n$, but not guaranteed.  The report therefore treats them as
random-pair experiments, not formal non-isomorphism certificates.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The current project should be viewed as a research prototype.  Its value is
that it makes graph isomorphism visible as an optimization problem: the soft
matrix $X^*$, the objective components, and the rounded certificate all expose
where the method succeeds or fails.

The strongest claim is therefore precise: adding the degree-profile term
improves the relaxed objective's separation over an adjacency-only baseline,
while exact certification remains dependent on the rounding stage.  For
friendly graphs, the relaxation is tight and the method certifies isomorphism
exactly.  For symmetric or weakly identifiable graphs, the relaxation gap and
rounding quality remain the fundamental bottlenecks.
